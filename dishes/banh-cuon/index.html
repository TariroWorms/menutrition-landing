🥟 Bánh cuốn (steamed rice rolls)

Origin: Vietnam · Hanoi and the North

Bánh cuốn are paper-thin sheets of steamed rice batter wrapped around a savoury filling of seasoned ground pork, wood ear mushroom and shallot. The rolls are silky, soft and almost translucent, topped with crispy fried shallots and herbs. They are served with a bowl of nước chấm, a sweet, sour and salty dipping sauce made with fish sauce and lime, usually for breakfast.

Recipe

4 servings60 min

Ingredients

  • 200 g fine ground rice (rice powder)
  • 30 g tapioca starch
  • 500 ml water and 1 tbsp oil for the batter
  • 250 g ground pork
  • 15 g dried wood ear mushrooms, soaked and finely chopped
  • 2 shallots, finely chopped
  • 4 tbsp fried shallots
  • For nước chấm: 3 tbsp fish sauce, 3 tbsp sugar, 3 tbsp lime juice, 150 ml water, 1 garlic clove, 1 small chilli
  • Fresh herbs such as mint and cilantro, salt and pepper

Method

  1. Whisk the ground rice, tapioca starch, water, oil and a pinch of salt into a thin batter and rest for 30 minutes.
  2. Fry the chopped shallots in a little oil, add the pork and wood ear, season with salt and pepper and cook until the pork is done.
  3. Brush a non-stick pan with oil, pour in a thin layer of batter, cover and steam over medium heat for about 1 minute until the sheet turns translucent.
  4. Slide the sheet onto an oiled board, spoon a line of filling along one edge and roll it up. Repeat with the rest of the batter.
  5. Mix the nước chấm ingredients, then serve the rolls topped with fried shallots and herbs, with the sauce for dipping.

How to make it lighter

  • Use lean ground pork and cook the filling with just a teaspoon of oil.
  • Fried shallots are tasty but rich, so scatter a small pinch instead of a heap.
  • Add a big plate of fresh herbs, cucumber and bean sprouts on the side to make the meal bigger without many extra calories.

History

Bánh cuốn is a speciality of northern Vietnam, especially Hanoi and the surrounding Red River Delta, where it has long been a popular breakfast. The sheets are traditionally steamed on a cloth stretched over a pot of boiling water, a technique still used by street vendors today.
